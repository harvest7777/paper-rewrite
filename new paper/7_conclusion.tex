\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Two coding agents, given an identical checkout of GraphViz and a prompt naming
no target, both located crossing minimization as the expensive phase and both
produced output-preserving improvements to it. Finding the bottleneck was
evidently not the difficult part of the task.

What separated them was breadth. Claude carried on into network simplex, which
serves two further phases, and improves three of five on every shape we
tested --- $1.33\times$ to $1.84\times$ end-to-end. Codex optimized the one
phase it identified and measures $1.29\times$ down to $0.99\times$. On the
sparsest shape, Codex's optimization works, at $1.31\times$ on its phase, and
still yields nothing end-to-end because that phase is $11.5\%$ of the workload.

The ceiling an optimization can reach is a property of the input, not of the
program. At fixed node count, crossing minimization ranges from $11.5\%$ to
$64\%$ of layout time across our three shapes, and on the sparsest one
Claude exceeds what any crossing-minimization optimization could achieve. A
single benchmark graph cannot show this.

The results are modest and we have tried to report them at the size they are.
The protocol may be the more durable contribution: balance command ordering and
take the floor from the baseline's own spread across those orderings; keep the
evaluation harness out of the agents' reach; forbid the shortcuts that
manufacture speedups; measure phases directly rather than by subtraction. Each
caught something a simpler design would have reported as a finding.

Immediate future work is to repeat both agent runs to establish whether the
ordering is stable, extend to a second graph size and to non-synthetic inputs,
and complete the correctness checks named in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.
