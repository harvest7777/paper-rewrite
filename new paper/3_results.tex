\subsection{Noise floor}
\label{sec:floor}

Because command order rotates, the baseline is measured once in each position.
Table~\ref{tab:floor} reports how far apart its four means land --- the
apparent speedup this protocol produces when nothing has changed.

Worked example, crossing minimization on sparse-deep at 5{,}000 nodes: the four
per-ordering means are $60.96$, $61.36$, $61.67$ and $60.18$\,ms, giving six
pairwise ratios between $1.005$ and $1.025$. The largest is the cell.

The floor is not uniform, which is why we report it per graph, per phase and per
size. At 5{,}000 nodes the noisiest cell is coordinate assignment on
dense-shallow at $1.155\times$, and it is not a property of that phase: the
fourth ordering measures $295.0$\,ms against $255.4$, $259.5$ and $260.1$ in the
other three, and the same ordering also inflates that graph's end-to-end figure.
One slow ordering raises every cell it touches.

Which graph is noisiest is not stable either. Dense-shallow has the worst
end-to-end floor at 5{,}000 nodes ($1.069\times$) and the best at 10{,}000
($1.015\times$), while default goes the other way, from $1.033$ to
$1.089\times$. Nothing about a shape makes it intrinsically noisy; a floor
describes the invocation it was measured in. That is the argument for measuring
one inside every experiment rather than carrying a number across experiments, or
across sizes.

Six ratios per cell is still a small sample, so each is a rough bound. That is
the cost of not conflating graphs.

\textbf{Throughout the rest of this section, we treat a measured ratio as a
change only if it exceeds the floor for that same phase and that same graph.}
Ratios that do not are marked in the tables that follow.

This floor is measured inside the same \texttt{hyperfine} invocations as the
results it bounds, on the same graphs, against the same competing commands. The
more conventional construction --- benchmarking separately-built identical
binaries --- places them in an invocation of their own, with a different command
set and thermal profile, and so describes a different experiment from the one
being guarded.

\subsection{Phase weights depend on shape, not size}
\label{sec:shape}

Which phase dominates is not fixed, and two things move it. At identical node
count, shape moves it the most: crossing minimization takes $12.4\%$ of layout
time on one shape and $64.8\%$ on another, while coordinate assignment moves the
other way, $44.3\%$ down to $9.2\%$. Size moves it too, and in a consistent
direction --- doubling to 10{,}000 nodes cuts crossing minimization's share on
every shape and hands the difference to network simplex. Each combination sets
its own ceiling, which is why one optimization gives different end-to-end
results on different graphs and at different sizes.

We report the unaccounted row rather than folding it into a phase. It is largest
on sparse-deep, where the layout work is smallest and the fixed cost of parsing
shows through, and it shrinks at 10{,}000 nodes on every shape because the
layout work grows while parsing and output do not.

Doubling the node count moves the weights in one direction on all three shapes.
Crossing minimization's share falls --- $12.4\%$ to $8.2\%$, $45.4\%$ to
$31.1\%$, $64.8\%$ to $52.3\%$ --- and network simplex takes what it gives up.
On sparse-deep, coordinate assignment goes from $44.3\%$ to $66.9\%$ of layout
time; on dense-shallow, ranking nearly doubles its share. Both network simplex
phases grow faster than linearly in node count while crossing minimization,
whose cost is dominated by within-rank comparisons at fixed rank width, grows
closer to linearly.

So composition is not a property of shape alone. At a fixed size, shape
separates the three workloads sharply; across sizes, every shape drifts the same
way. Any claim about which phase is worth optimizing has to name a size as well
as a shape.

At 5{,}000 nodes all four Claude runs improve all three shapes, from
$1.39\times$ to $2.91\times$, and every one of those twelve ratios clears its floor by a wide
margin. Among the four Codex runs, only the first shows any improvement at all:
$1.16\times$ on default and $1.33\times$ on dense-shallow, with sparse-deep
inside the floor. The other three Codex runs are indistinguishable from the
unmodified baseline on every graph.

That is not a failure to optimize. Three of those four Codex runs shipped no
layout code, by their own decision, and Section~\ref{sec:noship} treats them
separately --- both because averaging them with the first would describe a
binary nobody produced, and because a binary that is the baseline recompiled
tells us something useful about the floor.

The spread between repetitions of the same prompt is larger than we expected.
Claude's two base-prompt runs differ by $1.39$ against $1.67$, $1.55$ against
$1.98$, and $1.89$ against $2.35$ --- the second run is faster on every shape,
by more than the gap between some agents. Codex's two base-prompt runs differ
more sharply still: one shipped two changes, the other shipped nothing.

At 10{,}000 nodes every run that optimized network simplex improves by more
(Table~\ref{tab:endtoend10k}), which follows from the composition shift: the
phases these runs optimized are a larger share of a larger graph. The ordering
does not simply scale, though. Claude's second parallelism run is the fastest
binary we measured on dense-shallow at $4.02\times$, and the \emph{slowest} of
the four Claude runs on sparse-deep at $1.89\times$, where crossing
minimization --- the phase its concurrency targets --- is $8.2\%$ of the work.
The gap between the two base-prompt Claude runs widens with size: they are $1.39$
against $1.67$ at 5{,}000 on sparse-deep and $1.62$ against $2.53$ at
10{,}000.

Codex's one shipping run moves the other way. Its default-shape result falls
from $1.16$ to $1.14\times$ while the floor for that cell rises to
$1.089\times$, so it now barely clears, and its dense-shallow result falls from
$1.33$ to $1.24\times$. An optimization confined to crossing minimization is
worth less on bigger graphs, because that is the phase whose share shrinks with
size --- the ceiling it works under drops from $2.63$ to $2.04\times$ on that
shape.

Thirty-nine of the 75 entries exceed their floor, and they fall in an obvious
pattern: every Claude run on ranking, crossing minimization and coordinate

on all three. Initialization and spline routing never clear anywhere, which is
the control we expect --- no run touched either.

The four Claude runs divide the same three phases differently. Ranking is where
the parallelism-prompt runs separate themselves: $4.19$ to $4.59\times$ against
$2.07$ to $2.85\times$ for the base-prompt runs. Crossing minimization ranges
from $1.26$ to $4.31\times$, and coordinate assignment from $1.28$ to
$2.92\times$, with no run dominating the others on all three.

One entry is worth isolating. Claude's second parallelism run manages
$4.31\times$ on crossing minimization on dense-shallow but only $1.26\times$ on
sparse-deep --- the widest spread any run shows on any phase. Its main concurrency is
in \texttt{transpose}, which needs rank width to have anything to divide, and
sparse-deep is ten nodes wide.

Codex's first run improves crossing minimization by $1.32$ to $1.64\times$ and
nothing else. That is what produces its $1.03\times$ end-to-end on sparse-deep,
inside the floor: the optimization is real on the phase, but the phase is
$12.4\%$ of that workload.

At 10{,}000 nodes (Table~\ref{tab:phases10k}) the same pattern holds with every
effect larger. The two parallelism runs reach $5.21$ to $6.03\times$ on ranking,
against $2.15$ to $3.10\times$ for the base-prompt runs. Coordinate assignment
separates the runs most: Claude's second base-prompt run reaches $3.95$ to
$4.85\times$ there --- the best of any run on that phase --- while Claude's
first manages $1.34$ to $1.74\times$. Codex's shipping run still improves
crossing minimization and nothing else, now by $1.34$ to $1.61\times$.

Two entries move enough to be worth naming. Claude's second parallelism run goes
from $1.26$ to $1.58\times$ on crossing minimization on sparse-deep and from
$4.31$ to $5.10\times$ on dense-shallow, widening the gap its rank-width
dependence creates. And every run's coordinate-assignment speedup grows with
size, including the runs that ship no threads at all, which matters for
attributing the parallelism runs' gains below.

\subsection{The runs that shipped nothing}
\label{sec:noship}

Three of the four Codex runs changed no layout code at all. Their complete diff
against the starting commit is the report each was asked to write, plus, in one
case, an unused benchmark generator under \texttt{tools/}. Under \texttt{lib/}
and \texttt{cmd/} the diff is empty. Those three binaries are the baseline,
recompiled.

Each arrived there by a different route, and in each case by reverting work it
had already committed:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Codex 2} replaced the crossing counter with a Fenwick tree, then
      measured a $17\%$ regression at 25{,}000 nodes --- the shape the prompt
      named --- and reverted it. Its report attributes the loss to the original
      scan being cache-friendly at these rank widths.
\item \textbf{Codex 1\,P} parallelized adjacent-rank crossing counts with
      OpenMP, measured $0.92\times$ at two threads, and reverted.
\item \textbf{Codex 2\,P} did the same with pthreads, and reverted on the
      grounds that the phase it could parallelize was $0.2$\,s against a
      positioning phase of $1.7$ to $3.0$\,s.
\end{itemize}

We report these as deliberate outcomes, not as failed optimizations. Each run
built something, measured it against the workload the prompt described, and
concluded the change did not earn its place. The prompt explicitly allowed that
outcome.

They also give us something we did not design for: three binaries that are
byte-for-byte the baseline in every line of layout code, measured in the same
invocations as the rest, under different names and in different command
positions. Their nine end-to-end ratios run from $1.00$ to $1.03\times$, which
is what the floor predicts.

Their per-phase entries are less reassuring. All three measure $1.07$ to
$1.08\times$ on crossing minimization on sparse-deep and default --- above the
$1.025$ and $1.052$ floors for those cells --- and one measures $0.93\times$ on
coordinate assignment on default. Three independent binaries containing no
change do not agree on a $1.07\times$ improvement by chance, so this is a
position effect the baseline-against-itself construction does not capture: the
floor in Table~\ref{tab:floor} compares the baseline only with copies of itself
run in the same invocation, and cross-binary variation is evidently larger.

The practical consequence for this paper is small, because every result we claim
clears its floor several times over. But a per-phase ratio near $1.1\times$ in
this protocol should not be read as an effect, and we would not report one as
such.

\subsection{How much of the parallel gain is threads}
\label{sec:threads}

The two parallelism-prompt runs from Claude both ship thread pools, and both
choose their own thread count. Running them again at \texttt{GV\_THREADS=1}
separates what their concurrency buys from what their serial changes buy.
Figure~\ref{fig:threads} reports that measurement at both sizes, taken in its
own \texttt{hyperfine} invocations with two orderings and 20 timed runs per
configuration, so its ratios should be read against its own baselines ($578.2$,
$1368.3$, $3038.9$\,ms at 5{,}000 nodes; $1925.4$, $4406.7$, $10925.6$\,ms at
10{,}000) rather than against Tables~\ref{tab:endtoend}
and~\ref{tab:endtoend10k}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig5_thread_scaling.pdf}
\caption{Each binary at one thread and at its own default, as speedup over
baseline, at both node counts. The label above each pair is the default-thread
mean divided by the one-thread mean --- the part of the speedup that concurrency
accounts for. Claude 1\,P's threads are inert at 5{,}000 nodes and contribute at
10{,}000; Claude 2\,P's contribute wherever ranks are wide, at either size.}
\label{fig:threads}
\end{figure}

The two runs are not the same kind of result, despite landing close together in
Table~\ref{tab:endtoend}, and which one is a parallel result depends on the
input.

At 5{,}000 nodes, Claude's first parallelism run is a serial optimization that
also contains threads. At one thread it already reaches $1.61$, $1.99$ and
$2.33\times$; enabling its threads adds $1$ to $16\%$. Its report explains why:
most of its concurrency sits inside network simplex behind subtree-size thresholds of
32{,}768 and 16{,}384 nodes, and those paths rarely execute on a 5{,}000-node
graph. What it gained, it gained from rewriting subtree relabelling as array
operations.

At 10{,}000 nodes the same binary's threads begin to pay: $1.21$, $1.36$ and
$1.28\times$, against $1.01$, $1.05$ and $1.16\times$ at half the size. The
coordinate-assignment phase solves an auxiliary graph several times larger than
the input, so at this size it crosses the thresholds the agent chose. It is
still predominantly a serial result --- at one thread it reaches $1.91$ to
$2.71\times$ --- but the concurrency it shipped is no longer inert.

Claude's second parallelism run is the opposite at both sizes. At one thread it
is the weaker of the two everywhere. With threads it becomes the fastest binary
we measured, and on dense-shallow concurrency accounts for $2.32\times$ of its
result at 5{,}000 nodes and $2.78\times$ at 10{,}000. Its main threading is in
\texttt{transpose}, with a 2{,}000-node threshold every graph here clears, and
\texttt{transpose} is most of crossing minimization.

Sparse-deep separates the two designs most sharply, and it is the clearest
result in this section. At 10{,}000 nodes the first run gets $1.21\times$ from
threads there and the second gets $1.03\times$ --- the reverse of dense-shallow,
where they get $1.28$ and $2.78\times$. The first put most of its concurrency in network
simplex, which is $66.9\%$ of sparse-deep's runtime at that size; the second put
most of its in crossing minimization, which is $8.2\%$. Neither design is better. Each
pays exactly when the input makes its phase expensive, which is the same
composition argument that governs the serial results.

This is the measurement that decides how the parallelism prompt should be
described. It produced two runs that look equally parallel end to end, and
whether either deserves the label depends on the graph you hand it.

\subsection{Correctness}

All eight binaries produce \texttt{dot -Tdot} output byte-identical to the
baseline on every evaluation graph, at both node counts and with instrumentation
both enabled and disabled: 96 comparisons, no differences. The baseline
reproduces itself on all six graphs, so the comparison is meaningful on each.

We also ran the 260 graphs in GraphViz's own test suite through the five
binaries that shipped layout code. Every run changed \texttt{mincross.c}, which
only \texttt{dot} uses; the four Claude runs also changed \texttt{ns.c}, which
\texttt{neato} reaches as well, so we checked both engines. All 2{,}600
comparisons are identical, with matching exit codes and nothing skipped. We do
not report \texttt{fdp} or \texttt{circo}: the baseline \texttt{fdp} is not
reproducible against itself on these inputs, so byte comparison is meaningless
for it, and baseline \texttt{circo} does not finish one test graph within an
hour.

Two of the binaries create threads, so byte-identity at one thread count is not
sufficient for them. We repeated the whole test-suite comparison for both at
\texttt{GV\_THREADS} of 1, 2 and 8 and at their own default: 2{,}080 further
comparisons, all identical.

That check on its own would prove less than it appears to. Both binaries gate
their concurrency behind input-size thresholds --- 16{,}384- and 32{,}768-node
subtrees in one, 2{,}000 nodes in the other --- and almost every graph in
\texttt{tests/graphs} is far below all of them, so those 2{,}080 comparisons
largely exercise the serial paths. We therefore repeated the comparison on the
three evaluation graphs, which are large enough to execute the parallel code:
each binary at \texttt{GV\_THREADS} of 1, 2, 4, 8, 12 and its own default, three
repetitions each, for 108 comparisons. All are identical to the baseline.

Output does not depend on thread count in anything we ran, which is evidence for
the agents' determinism arguments but not a proof of race-freedom --- see
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

The remaining three binaries need no such check. Their layout sources are
identical to the baseline's (Section~\ref{sec:noship}), so their output is
identical by construction; they are nonetheless included in the 48 comparisons
above.

Reading all eight diffs against the prompt's disallowed shortcuts found no
reduced iteration counts, early exits, input special-casing, cached layout
results or modified tests. Two binaries do introduce concurrency, which the
parallelism prompt asked for and which the same prompt required to leave output
unchanged; the byte-identity results above are what we have to show for that
requirement.
