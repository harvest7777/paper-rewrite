\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Finding the bottleneck was not the hard part}

No run was told where to look, and none of the eight failed to find expensive
code. Every one profiled first, and every report names the same two regions:
crossing minimization, and the network simplex that serves both ranking and
coordinate assignment. Several runs independently arrived at the same
transformation --- fusing the two symmetric crossing-count passes into one.
Locating the work is evidently not what separates these runs.

Breadth is. All four Claude runs went into network simplex as well as crossing
minimization, and that is where their margin comes from: ranking and coordinate
assignment improve in every Claude run and in no Codex run
(Table~\ref{tab:phases}). Codex's one shipping run stopped at the phase it
identified first. The pattern holds across three workloads of very different
composition, so it is not an inference from a single measurement.

The reports also show the two agents disagreeing about the same code rather than
seeing different things. Both Codex parallelism runs argue that
\texttt{transpose} cannot be parallelized without changing output, because each
accepted swap changes the state the next comparison reads, and that network
simplex pivots are inherently sequential. Both Claude parallelism runs
parallelized exactly that code anyway --- one with a speculative sweep schedule
and an undo log, the other by running two independent ordering passes on private
copies --- and produced byte-identical output. The disagreement is not about
where the time goes. It is about whether a dependency is a hard barrier or an
engineering problem.

\subsection{Agents optimize the workload they imagine}

No run could see our evaluation graphs, so each generated its own. Every one
chose graphs larger than ours: 5{,}000 to 25{,}000 nodes, against our fixed
5{,}000.

That choice has consequences we can measure. Claude's first parallelism run put
its concurrency behind subtree-size thresholds of 32{,}768 and 16{,}384 nodes,
tuned on graphs where network simplex dominates. At 5{,}000 nodes those paths
almost never execute, and its threads contribute between $1$ and $16\%$
(Figure~\ref{fig:threads}) --- there it scores well on the strength of a serial
rewrite that happens to travel. Claude's second parallelism run put its
concurrency in \texttt{transpose} behind a 2{,}000-node threshold, which even our
smallest graphs clear, and its threads contribute up to $2.32\times$.

Neither run was tuned to our benchmark; one simply guessed a workload closer to
it. A study that reported only end-to-end numbers would have called both of them
parallel wins.

At 10{,}000 nodes the first run improves sharply --- $1.60$ to $2.38\times$ on
sparse-deep, $2.75$ to $3.46\times$ on dense-shallow --- and re-measuring it at
one thread shows where that came from. Its threads, worth $1$ to $16\%$ at
5{,}000 nodes, are worth $21$ to $36\%$ at 10{,}000
(Figure~\ref{fig:threads}). The concurrency it shipped was not useless; it was
written for graphs larger than the ones we first measured it on, and it switches
on when the input reaches the size it was tuned for. Most of its gain is still
serial even at 10{,}000 nodes, so the description of it as a serial optimization
that also contains threads survives --- but only as a statement about a size
range, not about the binary.

The reverse case is sharper. On sparse-deep at 10{,}000 nodes the first run's
threads are worth $1.21\times$ and the second run's $1.03\times$; on
dense-shallow the same comparison is $1.28$ against $2.78\times$. One agent put
its concurrency in network simplex and the other in crossing minimization, and
each is rewarded exactly on the shapes where its phase is expensive. Two agents
given the same instruction to use the idle cores produced designs whose value is
decided by the input, not by the instruction.

\subsection{Why breadth mattered: the ceiling moves with shape}

Amdahl's law~\cite{amdahl1967validity} bounds what optimizing one phase can
yield, and that bound depends on the phase's share of runtime --- which
Table~\ref{tab:composition} shows is a property of the graph's shape.

minus that phase's measured cost), i.e.\ the phase driven to zero. Remaining
columns are the end-to-end speedups from Tables~\ref{tab:endtoend}
and~\ref{tab:endtoend10k}, carrying the same dagger convention. Entries above
the ceiling are bold.}
\label{tab:ceiling}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Graph} & \textbf{Ceiling} & \textbf{Claude 1} & \textbf{Claude 2} & \textbf{Claude 1\,P} & \textbf{Claude 2\,P} & \textbf{Codex 1} \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{7}{l}{\emph{5{,}000 nodes}} \\
sparse-deep   & 1.12$\times$ & \textbf{1.39} & \textbf{1.67} & \textbf{1.60} & \textbf{1.47} & 1.03$^\dagger$ \\
default       & 1.72$\times$ & 1.55 & \textbf{1.98} & \textbf{2.10} & \textbf{2.19} & 1.16 \\
dense-shallow & 2.63$\times$ & 1.89 & 2.35 & \textbf{2.75} & \textbf{2.91} & 1.33 \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{7}{l}{\emph{10{,}000 nodes}} \\
sparse-deep   & 1.08$\times$ & \textbf{1.62} & \textbf{2.53} & \textbf{2.38} & \textbf{1.89} & 1.02$^\dagger$ \\
default       & 1.42$\times$ & \textbf{1.58} & \textbf{2.53} & \textbf{3.11} & \textbf{3.28} & 1.14 \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Codex's one shipping run sits below the ceiling everywhere, as a run that
optimized only that phase must. On sparse-deep at 5{,}000 nodes the ceiling is
$1.12\times$, so even a perfect crossing-minimization optimization would be
nearly invisible end-to-end. Its $1.03\times$ is not a failed optimization ---
the change works, at $1.32\times$ on the phase --- but the phase is worth
$12.4\%$ of that workload.

Every Claude run \emph{exceeds} that ceiling on sparse-deep, at both sizes,
which is only possible by improving other phases. That is the breadth argument
at its sharpest: on that workload no amount of further work on the phase every
run found could have matched what they did.

The ceiling also explains why the shapes rank the runs differently. On
dense-shallow at 5{,}000 nodes, where crossing minimization is $64.8\%$ of the
work, the ceiling is $2.63\times$ and only the two parallelism runs clear it ---
breadth matters less there, because the one phase everybody found is most of the
workload. On sparse-deep the ceiling is so low that breadth is the only thing
that matters.

Doubling the node count lowers every ceiling: $1.12$ to $1.08\times$, $1.72$ to
$1.42\times$, $2.63$ to $2.04\times$. Crossing minimization is a smaller share
of a larger graph, so the strategy of optimizing it alone is worth less exactly
where the absolute times are worst. Three Claude runs clear the dense-shallow
ceiling at 10{,}000 nodes where only two did at 5{,}000, and on default every
Claude run now clears a ceiling that has dropped below what three of them
already achieved. Breadth is not merely a way to go faster here; it is the only
strategy whose value grows with the problem.

A single benchmark graph would have hidden all of this --- and a single graph
\emph{size} would have hidden the direction it moves in.

The ceiling an optimization can reach is a property of the input, not the
program, and a single benchmark graph cannot show that.

The gap is consistent and large. Claude's runs took between $1$ and $5$ hours
and between $27$ and $92$ million tokens; Codex's took between $9$ and $24$
minutes and between $1.6$ and $5.1$ million. The cheapest Claude run cost
roughly five times the most expensive Codex run in tokens, and eight times in
wall time.

Both columns move together within each agent, and neither predicts the result.
Claude's most expensive run by tokens is its first, which is also its slowest
binary of the four; its longest run by time is the one that produced the fastest
binary. Codex's two cheapest runs shipped nothing, but so did its second run,
which cost twice as much.

These are not like-for-like counts. The two systems report different categories
--- one folds cached reads into a total that dwarfs everything else, the other
reports cache reads as a subset of input --- and a token is not a unit of work.
We report them because they are what the logs record, and because the
order-of-magnitude difference is too large to leave out, not because they
support a cost-per-speedup comparison.
