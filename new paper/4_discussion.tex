\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Both agents found the bottleneck}

Neither agent was told where to look, and both went to crossing minimization ---
the single largest consumer of runtime on the 5{,}000-node workload at $49.3\%$.
Both further converged on the same specific transformation, fusing the two
symmetric crossing-count passes, despite working in isolation. On this evidence
the bottleneck-finding half of the task is not the hard part.

The two diverge in how far they went. Codex stopped after optimizing the phase
it had identified. Claude continued into network simplex, which is used for both
ranking and coordinate assignment, and that decision is what its lead consists
of.

\subsection{The ceiling, and why breadth mattered}

Amdahl's law~\cite{amdahl1967validity} bounds what optimizing a single phase can
yield. From the composition in Table~\ref{tab:composition}, driving crossing
minimization to \emph{zero} cost caps end-to-end speedup at $1.97\times$ at
5{,}000 nodes --- and at only $1.60\times$ at 10{,}000, because that phase is a
smaller fraction of the larger workload.

That second number is the useful one. Claude measures $1.57\times$ at 10{,}000
nodes, which is essentially at the ceiling for crossing minimization alone. It
can sit there only because it also improved coordinate assignment; no amount of
additional work on crossing minimization could have taken it past $1.60\times$.
Codex, having modified one file, is bounded by a ceiling that shrinks as the
workload grows.

The general point is that on a multi-phase pipeline, the value of an
optimization depends on how the phase weights move with problem size. An agent
that optimizes the currently-dominant phase captures a ceiling that may be
receding. We would not have observed this with a single graph size, and we
suggest that phase-resolved measurement at more than one scale should be
standard in this kind of evaluation.

\subsection{Neither agent chose parallelism}

Given a free choice of method, both agents pursued serial algorithmic and
data-layout changes. Neither diff contains a single OpenMP directive or thread.

The ceiling arithmetic suggests this was correct rather than merely
conservative. Assume \emph{perfect} linear scaling across the 8 performance
cores --- an assumption crossing minimization's rank-by-rank sweeps would not
support. Parallelizing that phase alone then caps end-to-end speedup at
$1.76\times$ at 5{,}000 nodes and at $1.49\times$ at 10{,}000, the ceiling
falling as the phase's share of runtime does.

At 5{,}000 nodes Claude's serial $1.47\times$ is below that bound. At 10{,}000
it is above it: $1.57\times$ measured, against $1.49\times$ for idealized
eight-core parallelization of the phase both agents targeted. A serial change
outperformed what perfect parallelization of the nominated target could have
achieved --- without introducing concurrency, and therefore without introducing
the possibility of a data race.

This bears on the prior work discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related}, which
framed the problem as an OpenMP parallelization task and reported $3.78\times$.
That figure exceeds the $3.56\times$ ceiling for eliminating \emph{both} of the
two dominant phases entirely at 5{,}000 nodes, and is roughly twice what perfect
parallelization of its stated target could produce. We make no claim about how
that number arose; we observe that our measurements of the same codebase do not
support it, and that the phase composition bounds it.

\subsection{Cost}

The two runs differed substantially in resource consumption. Claude ran for
4\,h\,27\,m of working time and consumed roughly $9.2 \times 10^7$ tokens; Codex
ran 23\,m\,42\,s wall-clock and consumed roughly $5.1 \times 10^6$. That is more
than an order of magnitude in both dimensions for a $1.27\times$ difference in
delivered layout time at 5{,}000 nodes, widening to $1.36\times$ at 10{,}000.

We report these figures but caution against reading them as a like-for-like
comparison. The two systems account for tokens differently --- one folds cached
input into its input total, the other reports them separately --- and the time
figures are not the same measurement, one excluding time spent waiting on user
input and the other being raw elapsed time. Whether the additional effort was
\emph{necessary} for the additional result, or merely accompanied it, is not
something a single run of each can answer.
